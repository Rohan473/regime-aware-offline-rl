# 1. First: make the RL project interpretable

I would build an **“RL representation laboratory”** around your existing project.

Instead of asking only:

> What is the Sharpe?

ask:

> **What information is present in the hidden representation, what information is discarded, and what information is actually used to make the action?**

There are roughly five levels you can investigate.

---

## 2. Feature → representation → action decomposition

For every model, save:

$$
x_t \rightarrow h_t \rightarrow a_t
$$

where:

* \(x_t\) = input features
* \(h_t\) = hidden representation
* \(a_t\) = action

For DDR:

```text
8 features
   ↓
GRU
   ↓
hidden state h_t
   ↓
policy head
   ↓
position a_t
```

For TACR:

```text
20 × 8 feature sequence
        ↓
Transformer
        ↓
embedding h_t
        ↓
actor / critic
        ↓
position
```

Then you can investigate **what is encoded in \(h_t\)**.

This is much more interesting than simply looking at feature importance.

---

# 3. Linear probing is probably your highest-value experiment

Take frozen representations from the trained model.

For every timestep, extract:

$$
h_t
$$

Then train very simple models on \(h_t\).

For example:

### Probe A — future direction

Predict:

$$
Y^{dir}_{t+k} = \mathbb{1}[R_{t+k}>0]
$$

for:

* \(k=1\)
* \(k=5\)
* \(k=20\)

If a linear probe can predict future direction from the hidden state, then the representation contains directional information.

---

### Probe B — future volatility

Predict:

$$
\sigma_{t+5},\quad \sigma_{t+20}
$$

This asks whether the RL representation has learned volatility/regime information.

---

### Probe C — future absolute return

Predict:

$$
|R_{t+1}|
$$

This is especially interesting given your current result:

> confidence has almost no correlation with future absolute return.

You can test whether the **hidden state** contains information about magnitude even though the final policy does not use it.

---

### Probe D — regime

Predict:

```text
bull
bear
crisis
```

from \(h_t\).

If the model learns regimes, you should see high classification accuracy even if the final trading performance is mediocre.

---

### Probe E — behavior-policy identity

This one is particularly important for your offline RL setup.

Predict:

```text
momentum_30
momentum_40
...
mean_reversion_30
...
buy_and_hold
random
```

from the hidden state.

If this is highly predictable, the model may be learning:

> "What kind of trajectory generated this observation?"

rather than purely learning market structure.

That connects directly to one of your existing concerns about behavior-policy contamination.

# 4. The most interesting experiment: representation vs raw features

Don't just compare:

```text
raw features → prediction
```

with

```text
hidden representation → prediction
```

Compare:

### Model 1

Raw \(X\)

### Model 2

Hidden representation \(H\)

### Model 3

You can get results like:

| Information          | Direction | Volatility | Regime | Magnitude |
| -------------------- | --------: | ---------: | -----: | --------: |

That would tell a very different story from Sharpe.

> **The RL model transforms the input into a representation containing more regime information but not substantially more directional information.**

That's a genuinely interpretable ML result.

# 5. Measure feature redundancy explicitly

Your question about whether 8–16 fields are enough is excellent.

But don't answer it by saying:

> "8 features worked."

Instead ask:

> **How much unique information does each feature contribute?**

There are several ways to do this.

---

## A. Correlation matrix

Start simple.

Calculate:

$$
Corr(X_i,X_j)
$$

for all features.

But this is insufficient because nonlinear redundancy isn't captured.

For example:

```text
ret_20d
RSI
MACD
```

may have relatively low pairwise correlations while encoding broadly similar momentum information.

---

# 6. Conditional redundancy is much more interesting

For each feature \(X_i\), compare predictive performance:

### All features

$$
Y \sim X_{1:8}
$$

versus

### Remove feature i

$$
Y \sim X_{-i}
$$

Define:

$$
\Delta_i =
Performance(X_{1:8}) -
Performance(X_{-i})
$$

This gives you **unique predictive information**.

For example:

| Feature  | Full Sharpe | Without feature |     Δ |
| -------- | ----------: | --------------: | ----: |
| ret_1d   |        1.05 |            0.99 | +0.06 |
| ret_5d   |        1.05 |            1.03 | +0.02 |
| ret_20d  |        1.05 |            0.91 | +0.14 |
| RSI      |        1.05 |            1.04 | +0.01 |
| MACD     |        1.05 |            1.02 | +0.03 |
| volume z |        1.05 |            1.05 |  0.00 |

That immediately tells you whether 8 fields are actually 8 pieces of information.

---

# 7. Better: permutation importance

Train the model normally.

Then permute one feature while leaving everything else intact:

$$
X_i \rightarrow \pi(X_i)
$$

and measure:

$$
\Delta Performance_i
$$

Do this **out-of-sample**, not on the training data.

For RL, you can measure:

* action correlation
* action MSE
* Sharpe
* exposure
* turnover
* regime-specific Sharpe

The action-level metric is particularly useful:

$$
\Delta a_i =
E[(a_t-a_t^{(-i)})^2]
$$

This tells you:

> Which features actually cause the policy to change its decision?

---

# 9. The 8 → 16 experiment should become a proper scaling experiment

You already did:

```text
8 → 16
```

But that's only one point.

I would do:

$$
4 \rightarrow 8 \rightarrow 12 \rightarrow 16 \rightarrow 24 \rightarrow 32
$$

while keeping:

* dataset
* seeds
* architecture
* training budget
* optimizer
* evaluation
* transaction costs

identical.

Then plot:

```text
                  Performance
                       │
                       │        ●
                       │     ●
                       │   ●
                       │ ●
                       │●
                       └────────────────
                         4  8  16 24 32
                              Features
```

There are several possible outcomes.

### Case A — monotonic improvement

Then information was the bottleneck.

### Case B — plateau

Then the original 8 features were already sufficient.

### Case C — inverted U

This is particularly interesting:

> Additional features initially help, but eventually hurt because the RL objective struggles to learn from redundant/noisy dimensions.

### Case D — completely flat

This suggests the bottleneck isn't information.

And **your existing 8→16 TACR result is already pointing toward C/D**, rather than "we need more fields."

# 10. Measure information efficiency

You can go one step further.

Define something like:

$$
IE =
\frac{\text{out-of-sample performance}}
{\text{number of input dimensions}}
$$

But I'd actually prefer:

$$
\text{Marginal information efficiency}
=
\frac{\Delta Performance}{\Delta Features}
$$

Then compare RL models.

For example:

| Features | Sharpe | ΔSharpe |
| -------: | -----: | ------: |
|        4 |   0.72 |       — |
|        8 |   0.96 |   +0.24 |
|       16 |   0.97 |   +0.01 |
|       32 |   0.95 |   −0.02 |

That would give you a very clean conclusion:

> Most of the exploitable information is captured by the first eight variables.

# 12. Temporal representation experiments

Because this is financial time series, I would specifically investigate **what history the model actually uses**.

You have a 20-day context.

But does it need 20 days?

Run:

```text
context = 1
context = 3
context = 5
context = 10
context = 20
context = 40
context = 60
```

Then examine both performance **and representation**.

More importantly, mask different parts of the sequence:

```text
days -1 to -5
days -6 to -10
days -11 to -20
```

and see how much the action changes.

Define:

$$
I_k =
E[(a_t-a_t^{mask(k)})^2]
$$

You effectively obtain a **temporal receptive field of the trading policy**.

You might find:

> Although TACR receives 20 days, 80% of its action variance comes from the last 3 days.

That's a much more interpretable result.

# 14. Counterfactual policy analysis

This is probably the most intuitive interpretability experiment.

Take a real state:

```text
ret_1d = -2%
ret_5d = -4%
vol = high
RSI = low
...
```

and perturb one feature:

```text
ret_1d = -2% → +2%
```

Then measure:

$$
\Delta a = a(x')-a(x)
$$

Repeat across thousands of observations.

You can estimate:

$$
\frac{\partial a}{\partial x_i}
$$

or nonlinear response curves.

For each feature, generate:

> **What does this RL policy do when this variable increases while everything else stays fixed?**

That gives you policy response curves.

# 19. Idea 3 — Representation collapse in financial RL

This is potentially very interesting given your TACR behavior.

Measure effective dimensionality of \(h_t\).

For example, calculate the covariance:

$$
C = Cov(H)
$$

and its eigenvalues:

$$
\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\ldots,\lambda_d
$$

Then calculate effective rank:

$$
r_{eff}
=
\exp\left(
-\sum_i p_i\log p_i
\right)
$$

where

$$
p_i=\frac{\lambda_i}{\sum_j\lambda_j}.
$$

If a 128-dimensional Transformer embedding has effective rank of, say, 7, then the model isn't really using a 128-dimensional representation.

That would be fascinating.

You could compare:

```text
GRU 32D → effective rank 8
TACR 128D → effective rank 6
```

Potential conclusion:

> High-capacity financial RL networks may compress market states into a very low-dimensional representation.

# 21. Idea 5 — Temporal leakage / memorization vs generalization

Because offline financial RL can be extremely vulnerable to memorizing trajectory patterns, deliberately destroy temporal structure.

For example:

### Experiment A

Original sequence.

### Experiment B

Shuffle observations within regime.

### Experiment C

Shuffle observations globally.

### Experiment D

Preserve marginal distributions but destroy temporal dependencies.

Then train the same model.

If performance remains surprisingly similar after destroying temporal structure, the model isn't exploiting the temporal dynamics you thought it was.

This is a powerful sanity check.

# 22. Idea 6 — Causal feature substitution

This one is particularly finance-specific.

Take a feature:

```text
RSI
```

Replace it with an independent variable having the **same distribution**.

If performance doesn't change, RSI wasn't contributing information.

Do this systematically.

You can create:

### Null feature

Same mean/std/distribution, randomized temporal order.

### Autocorrelated null

Preserves time-series autocorrelation but contains no predictive relationship.

### Factor-matched null

Preserves correlation with other features but destroys future predictiveness.

Then compare.

This separates:

> distributional information

from

> temporal information

from

> genuinely predictive information.

# 23. Idea 7 — Can the model identify when its information is insufficient?

Your uncertainty hypothesis was null, but that doesn't mean uncertainty is a dead research area.

You tested ensemble disagreement.

A better question is:

> **Can representation geometry identify states where the model is outside its training distribution?**

Calculate distance from current \(h_t\) to the training representation distribution:

$$
D(h_t,\mathcal H_{train})
$$

using:

* Mahalanobis distance
* kNN distance
* density estimation
* energy score

Then test whether large representation distance predicts:

* sign error
* drawdown
* action instability
* poor future Sharpe

This is **representation-based OOD detection**, rather than generic ensemble uncertainty.

That is much more defensible.

# 29. And this changes how I'd interpret your current null result

I wouldn't conclude:

> "Trading decision decomposition is falsified."

That's too strong.

Your evidence supports something more precise:

> **In this experimental setting, jointly learning the full continuous trading action did not produce measurable incremental value over a decomposed supervised-direction + sizing construction.**

Your existing paper already appropriately says that decomposition isn't universally proven; it is conditional on the tested setting.

The more interesting unresolved question is **why**.

There are at least four competing explanations:

```text
                   Joint RL fails
                        │
        ┌───────────────┼────────────────┐
        ↓               ↓                ↓
 insufficient       objective       representation
 information        mismatch        problem
        │               │                │
     8 → 16           DSR vs Q       bad encoding
        │                                │
        └───────────────┬────────────────┘
                        ↓
                 sample inefficiency
```

And your current 8→16 experiment is already evidence **against a simple "not enough features" explanation**: adding the eight macro/cross-asset variables barely changes TACR's negative margin.

So rather than adding more and more financial features, I would now ask:

> **What information does each architecture actually retain in its latent state?**

That is the natural next chapter of this project.


The key is that **you don't need to abandon the existing project**. The existing null results actually make these experiments more compelling: you've established that adding information alone doesn't rescue end-to-end RL, so now you can investigate **information utilization and representation formation** rather than just continuing to search for a better Sharpe.
